Kobido Massage

Japanese facial massage as bodywork

Kobido is a Japanese approach to facial massage characterised by a structured sequence of techniques and continually changing rhythms.

Some parts are quick, precise and stimulating; others are slower, deeper and quieter. The session works primarily with the face, head and neck, creating an experience that can move between intensity, softness and deep relaxation.

Kobido is often associated with beauty and “anti-ageing”. That isn’t the main focus of my practice.

I’m more interested in what happens when we approach the face as part of the body — something we can experience through touch and sensation, rather than only through how it looks.

A massage with changing rhythms

What makes Kobido particularly distinctive is its variety.

Rather than repeating one type of movement throughout the session, the practice moves through different techniques, pressures and speeds. At one moment the work can be dynamic and technically precise; at another, slow and almost still.

The method I practise incorporates five elements:

Deeper tissue work

Slower, more focused techniques work with the face, jaw, neck and head through kneading, compression and sustained pressure.

This allows more time for areas that feel tense or simply benefit from slower attention. “Deep” doesn’t mean painful — the intensity is adapted to you.

Relaxation

The rhythm becomes slower and the movements softer and more repetitive.

Gentle strokes and rhythmic movements across the face, head and neck create a quieter counterpoint to the more active parts of the massage.

Drainage

Light, directional movements follow pathways commonly used in facial lymphatic drainage, moving across the face and towards the neck.

Dynamic lifting techniques

Quick kneading, rolling, percussion and other manual techniques create one of the liveliest and most recognisable parts of Kobido. These techniques are traditionally connected with the cosmetic dimension of the practice, but they also introduce a very different rhythm and sensory quality into the session.

Acupressure

Focused pressure is applied to particular points of the face and head and held briefly.

It introduces another shift in pace: from continuous movement towards moments of stillness and concentrated pressure.

The face as part of the body

We use our faces constantly.

We speak, eat, look, react, concentrate, communicate and express ourselves through them. The jaw, eyes, forehead, scalp and neck are involved in countless small actions throughout the day — yet we rarely pay attention to what these areas actually feel like.

This is what interests me about facial massage.

Rather than treating the face primarily as something to improve visually, Kobido can become an opportunity to bring sensation and attention to an unusually expressive and sensitive part of the body.

And because the face doesn’t exist separately from the rest of us, working with it can feel surprisingly whole-body.

Beyond appearance

You may notice that your face looks different after a session. Kobido has a long association with facial care and its dynamic techniques are often used with cosmetic intentions.

For me, the more interesting question is not only:

How does my face look? but also: How does my face feel?

Perhaps softer. More relaxed. More noticeable. Or simply different from when you arrived. That shift in attention is an important part of why I practise Kobido as bodywork for the face, head and neck.

What does research say?

Scientific research specifically on Kobido remains limited, so I prefer not to make claims that the available evidence cannot support.

There is broader research on facial massage, massage and structured touch, including studies investigating relaxation, mood, circulation and physiological responses to massage. This research can provide useful context, but findings from general facial massage or massage therapy shouldn’t automatically be interpreted as evidence for specific effects of Kobido.

For those interested in the research, I’ve collected some of the studies and reviews:

  • Facial massage, mood and anxiety
    A 2008 study examined 32 healthy women before and after a 45-minute facial massage. Immediately afterwards, participants had lower scores for anxiety and negative mood. The researchers also observed a change in one measure of autonomic nervous-system activity. Hatayama et al., 2008 — PubMed
  • Research involving Kobido techniques
    Kobido facial massage may improve facial appearance by reducing muscle tension, smoothing skin folds, and improving facial contours. It may also enhance blood and lymphatic circulation in the face and head. Leszczyńska & Zaleski, 2024 — Full article
  • Facial massage and blood flow
    A 2018 study found that a short facial massage with a roller temporarily increases blood flow to the skin. Using a facial roller regularly over a longer period may also help the blood vessels widen more easily, improving circulation. Miyaji et al., 2018 — PubMed
  • Massage and physiological measures associated with stress
    Massage therapy can temporarily reduce physiological signs of stress, particularly cortisol levels and heart rate after a single session. Moraska et al., 2010 — Full review on PMC
  • Facial massage and ageing
    Research and early findings suggest that regular physical activity and manual lymphatic drainage may help prevent skin aging by safely supporting the skin’s structure and healthy function. Nowakowska et al., 2025 — Full review

What to expect?

A Kobido session takes place on a massage table. The massage works primarily with the face, scalp, head and neck, without and with oil. Because Kobido includes both slower and more dynamic techniques, the sensation and rhythm change considerably throughout the session.
